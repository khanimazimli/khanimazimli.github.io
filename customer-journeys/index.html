From sending campaigns to deciding who should hear what.

A bank can message any customer at any time. The harder, more valuable question is whether it should: who qualifies, when the moment is right, and when the better decision is to stay silent.

01

Executive overview

Central question: how do we move from sending campaigns to making smarter customer decisions?

The bank's hyper-personalization squad turns behavioural and transactional data into personalized offers and journeys in the mobile app. I work on the customer side of that system: understanding what customers are doing, defining which of them a campaign is for, designing the sequence of messages, and taking each campaign through a five-stage process, including Risk and Finance approval, to launch.

This case shows how I structure those decisions, where my part ends and other teams' begin, and the real status of the campaigns I owned. It includes one supporting example, a weather-triggered journey, which is still in development.

02

Business problem

Many teams want the same customer's attention. Sending everything is not personalization.

Deposits, cards, investments, loyalty and partner offers each have their own targets. Left alone, every team reaches for the same active customers through the same channels. The result is noise: more messages, less relevance, and offers that arrive when they no longer fit.

The business needed three things from a personalization squad:

  • Relevance: offers tied to something the customer has actually done or is about to do.
  • Restraint: rules that keep the wrong customers out and stop the same segment receiving conflicting messages.
  • Accountability: a governed flow where every campaign is approved, built and measured the same way.

Every campaign moves through five governed stages, each with one owning team:

  • Several approval points for each campaign, including Risk and Finance, with every customer-facing text prepared in Azerbaijani, English and Russian before build starts.
  • A scoring engine owned by Data Science, which already applies bank-wide filters such as do-not-contact lists, recently contacted customers and control groups.
  • Platform change. In May 2026 a content-system migration blocked several launches, so plans had to be resequenced.

05

How I approach a campaign decision

  1. Name the business problemStart from what the bank needs to change, such as product take-up, spending on the bank's cards or engagement with the app, not from the channel.
  2. Understand the behaviourFind the customers whose actions already point at the need, and the moment it shows: a salary arriving, a spending pattern, an upcoming event.
  3. Define the decision criteriaWrite eligibility as rules a platform can execute: a spend threshold within a period, a product just taken, a time window, a premium segment's birthday.
  4. Structure the journeyDecide the order, timing and channel of each message, and what ends the journey for a customer.
  5. Coordinate requirementsAgree rules, wording and reward costs with Marketing, Risk and Finance before anything is built.
  6. Test the scenariosWalk edge cases with the engagement team: customers who qualify twice, who are already in another campaign, or whose data is incomplete.
  7. Improve the experienceReview results and customer feedback, then tighten or loosen the rules.

One rule from my own process notes captures the restraint side: never send mixed communications to the same segment at the same time. If two squads target the same customers in the same week, the later campaign moves, even when both are approved.

    Supporting example: a weather-triggered journey

    What if the right moment is not in the customer's data at all, but outside, in the weather?

    Most triggers come from a customer's own transactions. I designed a campaign scenario that uses a weather forecast instead: the forecast selects the day the offer runs, messages are sequenced ahead of that day, and participation is capped so the same customer is not rewarded repeatedly. The theme is a seasonal dining offer for cold weather.

    It shows the approach well because most of the design effort went into the rules: what counts as qualifying weather, how far ahead to message, what happens if the forecast changes, and how to stop it from becoming a weekly promotion.

    09

    Lessons and transferable capabilities

    Ideas become useful as executable rules.A campaign idea becomes useful when it can be stated as criteria a platform executes and a risk team can approve.
    Holding customers back protects relevance.Rules that hold customers back, such as participation limits and launch sequencing, protect the relevance of every other message.
    Know where your ownership ends.In a regulated bank, good work depends on clean hand-offs to Data Science, the engagement team, Risk and Finance.
    Design measurement in.Control groups and clear success criteria have to be part of the scenario, not added after launch.
