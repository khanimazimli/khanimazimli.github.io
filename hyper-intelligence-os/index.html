Hyper Intelligence OS: AI agents prepare, people decide.

Preparing one personalized campaign takes six separate steps, several teams and weeks of manual hand-offs. I started a prototype that joins those steps into one system, without taking any decision away from the people accountable for it.

01

Executive overview

One sentence in. A structured brief, a journey document, channel copy and visual drafts out, each waiting for a human decision.

Hyper Intelligence OS is a prototype for preparing personalized banking campaigns. A campaign idea written in one sentence becomes a structured brief, a customer-journey and scenario document, channel copy checked against the brief, and visual drafts generated from a creative brief. Specialized AI steps do the preparation; people approve or send the work back at every stage.

It is not a production system. It runs as a working prototype outside the bank's live infrastructure, and it has not been used on a live campaign.

02

The problem

Six steps, many teams, manual coordination.

From my own squad's work, a single campaign passes through six stages owned by different teams. Added up end to end, the typical durations come to about 12–24 working days. That is a simplified sum: some work runs in parallel, and it is not a measured average.

03

Worked analysis: where should AI help, and where must it not?

Business questionWhere does the time to prepare one campaign go, and which steps can be accelerated without weakening control in a regulated bank?
HypothesisMost of the elapsed time sits in steps that restate the same facts in a new format (idea, brief, scenario, copy, visual brief), not in the decisions themselves. Those steps can be assisted; the decisions cannot.
Evidence usedStage-by-stage duration estimates and owning teams from my squad's campaign process, and the approval points each campaign must pass. These are working estimates, not measured averages.
ApproachScore each stage on two axes, its share of elapsed time and the risk of automating it, then build where time is high and decision risk is manageable.

Options compared.

OptionForAgainst
Process fixes only: templates and deadlinesCheap, no new toolsStill asks people to restate the same facts five times
Automate end to end, approvals includedFastest on paperRemoves the controls the bank is accountable for
A generic AI writing toolAvailable todayNo link between brief, scenario and copy, so numbers can drift between steps

Decision and rationale. Build the four preparation modules in order of dependency (brief first, because every later step reuses its facts), keep Risk and Finance review human by design, and leave platform configuration for a later integration. At the midpoint estimate, that targets roughly three quarters of the elapsed time without touching a single approval.

How the outcome will be measured. There is no outcome yet: the prototype has not run on a live campaign. A pilot would compare, campaign by campaign:

  • elapsed working days per stage, against the current estimates;
  • review rounds per copy and visual before approval;
  • number mismatches between brief and copy caught before review;
  • generation cost per campaign, against the cost of the creative it replaces;
  • and, as a guardrail, no change in the time or depth of Risk and Finance review.
04

My initiative

I started this on my own initiative, because the same preparation work repeated on every campaign: restating the brief, rewriting the scenario in another format, adapting copy for each channel, briefing visuals from scratch. Most of it was translation between formats, not judgement.

My aim was narrow: remove the translation work, keep the judgement. That set three design rules from the start:

AreaMy contribution
InitiativeStarted it on my own, outside any assigned project, to address a problem I saw in my squad's daily work.
Product ownershipDecided the scope, the order of modules, and which steps stay human by design.
Workflow designDesigned the target flow: which AI step prepares what, and where a person approves or sends work back.
Hands-on buildWrote the campaign skills and prompt specifications the modules run on, and built the prototype with an AI coding assistant, testing it in the browser as I went.

Not claimed: use on live campaigns, a formal demo to management, or measured savings. None of these has happened yet.

  • The brief step does not invent facts. It extracts only what the person wrote; anything missing is asked, not assumed.
  • People keep every decision. Risk and Finance review stays human. Every draft can be approved or sent back with a comment.
  • Cost is controlled. Paid image generation runs only after an explicit human consent; reusing an approved creative is the default.

    07

    How I built it

    1. Mapped the real workflowListed every stage of campaign preparation from my own squad's work: who does it, how long it takes, and what is judgement versus reformatting.
    2. Drew the line between AI and peopleDecided which steps AI may prepare and which stay human by design: approvals, Risk and Finance, final creative choice, activation.
    3. Built module by moduleStarted with the brief, because every later step depends on it, then the journey document, copy and visuals.
    4. Kept deterministic where possibleBrief parsing, financial calculations, document generation and package QA are rule-based. Language models are used where writing and creative judgement help.
    5. Tested in a browser, repeatedlyEach module was tested with automated browser runs throughout development. These are development tests, not live usage.
    6. Iterated on feedbackCopy tone was the weakest part: early drafts did not always match the bank's voice. Tone rules are now being refined with Marketing, and a human reviewer can steer each variant.
    08

    Practical value

    The potential benefits are operational. None of them has been measured in production yet:

    • Shorter preparation. The four built modules (three working, one in testing) cover the stages that take most of the 12–24 days today: brief, journey, copy and visuals.
    • Fewer reformatting errors. Numbers in the copy are checked against the brief automatically, so a 10% offer cannot become 15% in a push.
    • A visible decision trail. Each approval or rejection is recorded with a comment, which makes governance easier to audit.
    • Lower creative cost. Reusing approved creatives is the default route; paid generation is a deliberate, approved choice.
    What I do not claim. No time saved, cost saved or campaign performance is claimed here, because the system has not run on live campaigns.

    10

    Lessons and transferable capabilities

    Automate translation, not judgement.The useful question was not “what can AI do?” but “which steps are reformatting work, and which are decisions?”
    Deterministic checks around the facts.Rule-based parsing and checks handle numbers and conditions; language models handle wording. That split is what makes the output checkable.
    Approval points made it easier to explain.Approval points, comments and cost consent made the prototype easier to explain to a regulated organisation, not harder.
    Say exactly how mature it is.Separating working, testing and planned parts is what lets a prototype be taken seriously.
